Why your car meet photos look flat (and the fix)

Most UK car meets happen on grey Sunday mornings. The cars look great in person, then the photos come out dark, grey and dull. This happened to me for a long time. Here is why it happens and how I fix it in Lightroom, step by step, with my own photos from real meets.

1. Why it happens

Three things add up on a grey morning:

  1. No shadows. A cloudy sky lights the car from everywhere at once. There are no hard shadows, so the curves of the body don't show.
  2. The sky decides the exposure. The sky is the brightest part of the frame. The camera exposes so the sky isn't blown out, and the cars, people and tarmac come out dark.
  3. Raw files start flat on purpose. A raw file keeps as much detail as it can. It looks low in contrast and slightly grey until you edit it. That's normal, not a mistake.

Put together, you get the "before" photos below: dark, grey and lifeless, even though the meet was great.

2. Before you edit

  • Shoot raw if your camera can. You can still fix a JPEG, but a raw file gives you much more room to lift dark areas without the colours breaking up.
  • Don't fight the grey sky. On a flat morning I keep the sky out of the frame where I can, and shoot lower and closer to the car. Less sky means the camera exposes for the car.
  • Edit on a screen at normal brightness. A phone at full brightness hides how dark the photo really is.

3. The fix, in six steps

These are the panels in Lightroom (Classic, desktop or mobile). The same sliders exist in Camera Raw and most other editors. I don't give fixed numbers, because every photo needs different amounts. Move each slider until the photo looks right, then move it a little back.

Step 1: White balance

Grey light often turns the whole photo blue-grey. Use the white balance picker on something that should be neutral: a grey number plate surround, a white car, a concrete kerb. Then nudge Temp warmer until white paint looks white, not blue.

Step 2: Exposure

Raise Exposure until the car looks the way you remember it. Ignore the sky for now. It's fine if the sky goes very light: a pale sky reads as a soft morning.

Step 3: Highlights and shadows

Pull Highlights down to bring back detail in the sky and on bright paint. Lift Shadows a little to open the wheel arches, tyres and the hedge behind. Too much shadow lift looks fake, so stop early.

Step 4: Contrast, with the tone curve

This is where flat becomes punchy. Add a little Contrast, or use the Tone Curve: a gentle S shape (darken the low part, brighten the high part) gives the body back its shape. Then raise the Blacks end of the curve a touch so the darkest areas aren't pure black. That keeps the soft, misty feel of a morning.

Step 5: Colour (HSL)

Grey mornings make greens and yellows look muddy. In HSL / Color:

  • Lower the saturation of green and yellow a little, so hedges and trees stop pulling the eye.
  • Leave red almost alone. Red paint should stay the colour of the car. Pushing red saturation turns it orange or pink and it stops looking real.
  • Keep skin natural. If faces go orange, lower orange saturation.

Step 6: Texture, clarity and crop

A small amount of Texture brings out paint, tarmac and wet details. Go easy on Clarity: too much gives a hard, crunchy HDR look. Last, crop so the car fills more of the frame and the grey sky takes up less.

That's the whole method: warm it up, brighten the car, bring back the sky, give it shape, calm the greens, keep the reds true.

5. Mistakes I made

  • Fixing the sky first. I used to pull the exposure down to save the sky, and the car stayed dark. Fix the car first, then bring the sky back with Highlights.
  • Too much saturation. It makes a grey morning look like a cartoon, and red cars go orange.
  • Crushed blacks. Pure black shadows make a misty morning look like night. Lift the blacks slightly.
  • A different look on every photo. A set from one meet looks better when every photo is edited the same way. Edit one, then copy the settings to the rest and adjust exposure only.
